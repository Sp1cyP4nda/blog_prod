### Digital Hygiene
This is basically going to be another round of practices and tools in the form of a list. The previous tools was my attempt at decent beginner-friendly tools that largely offer a free-tier of usage that you can start with to see if the tools, and honestly the lifestyle in general, are for you. These tools, on the other hand, won't necessarily have free-tier and may require a bit more willingness to expand your understanding of toolsets, but I still want the buy-in to be pretty cheap at this point, financially and technically. - Let's start with your [[Networking Overview|network]]. Did you know that the box your internet service provider (ISP; e.g. Xfinity, Verizon, etc.) gives you that "makes internet" doesn't belong to you? You're renting it. Look on your bill; you'll see a charge for like $10/$20+ bucks a month. Even worse: any device directly connected to this box, either physically (ethernet) or wirelessly (wifi), is visible to your ISP! And, there are no laws stopping them from selling what they can see. Which is more bogus of them making money from us _and_ selling our data. Awesome. Get rid of their device. Pick up a cheap modem that can handle the speeds you pay for and put a router behind it. Your ISP can still see the modem, but nothing else (-ish, to be discussed later).
- FOSS is your friend. The internet (and software in general) is held together with wires and duct tape. Companies take freeware, slap on extra "features" (that no one wants, and don't work), then sell it. Some companies sell things that are worth buying, other's don't. FOSS is Free and Open-Source Software. Free, in this case, does not necessarily mean monetarily.
	- Free refers to the "Four Essential Freedoms" of The Free Software Definition by Richard Stallman: the freedom to run a program for any purpose, the freedom to modify and study how the program works, the freedom to redistribute copies to help others, and the freedom to distribute copies of your modified version to others, to benefit the whole community from your changes. These freedoms also presuppose that you make your changed source code available for others to be able to modify and study your new source code.
	- If "free" refers to the convention and practice of the "Four Essential Freedoms," open-source refers to the legal licensing with which computer software is released that legally grants users the rights to use, study, modify, and distribute the software and its source code to anyone and for any purpose.
- Your phone is always watching. Locking down your phone and why to do so can take up an entire post to itself. So, I'll be brief here. Just like with all devices, keep your phone up to date, use strong passwords, and backup your data to a secured location. For phone-specific advice, turn off notification previews, get a second device if you need to use data broker apps, like PayPal or SnapChat, use secure messaging apps and encourage your social circle to do the same, use an alternative OS, like [GrapheneOS](https://grapheneos.org/install/) or LineageOS, and finally, be mindful of cloud uploads. Uploading to the cloud is marketing-speak for uploading your files, images, and videos to someone else's computer and trusting they won't sell it (spoiler: they do, and you signed a ToS that legally allows them to). There's more to do, but this is a solid start. I'll cover more phone lockdown later.
- Speaking of phones, let's talk burner devices? Put simply, a burner device is a cheap device, phone, laptop, etc. that can be disposed of at the drop of a hat. There are many ways to actually track a burner phone to you, so there are a few rules to follow. These are not guidelines. Breaking any one of these can burn the device before you even purchase it. Also, to properly set expectations, this list will not cover how to hide from well-resourced tracers.
	1. Cash only. Do not purchase anything with plastic.
	2. While your at it, don't purchase anything with plastic near or on the way to the store.
	3. Be mindful of cameras. Don't make the mistake of allowing yourself to be tracked between your home and the store.
	4. Use a cutout. Hire someone to enter the store and purchase the phone on your behalf.
	5. Leave your phone at home. Your phone is tracking you. Leave it home.
	6. After you have the device, never cross streams. Keep the burner off until necessary, and never near your home or other devices
- Regularly review devices for cleanup. Be honest with yourself, when was the last time you opened some of the things you've downloaded to your phone, your computer, your whatever. Once a month, once a season, twice a year. Set a schedule for review of your devices. Review accounts, apps, software, games, and permissions. If you haven't used something in a while, consider why you are keeping it. Some things just don't get used very often. That's fine, keep those things. Trash the rest. If you need them again, just download them when needed.
- Default settings benefit the company, not you. When you first get a device, download a new app, or open a new online account, go through all the settings on that device, app, or account and audit how things are set and marvel at the deception that is default settings. You should also do this periodically as "features" are added that get their own default settings that harvest your data.
- Don't blindly click on links you find on the internet, in your inbox, or sent to your phone. Hover over the link first to see where it goes. In a browser, the location usually appears in the bottom left corner. In an app, it should appear under your cursor. On mobile, long hold on the link to bring up a secondary menu that allows you to edit, share, or open the link. As you see more links, you'll get better at learning how to spot fakes e.g., g.oogle.com instead of google.com. When in doubt, just don't click the link. Pay attention to the address bar at the top of your browser.
### Encryption
So, this is where things start to get a bit technical. But, if you want to protect yourself, you have to learn some of the underlying technologies that power privacy, security, and the tools that make this possible to make educated decisions down the road. Plus, you've already read this far, I may as well actually teach something.

Encryption – or more specifically called cryptographic solutions, aka crypto (no, not the currency) – is an incredibly wide net that is at the core of security, hence why I'm starting here. There are many cryptographic solutions, from PKI, digital signatures, and certificates to obfuscation, salting, and hashing. These are not basic, so I will cover them in a later post.

Yes, I did use an en-dash. No, this wasn't written by AI. Although, I did learn they existed and how to use them because of AI.

Narrowing encryption down even further, there are a few categories that would be helpful to outline for the purposes of this post; asymmetric vs symmetric encryption, encryption algorithms and key length, and levels of encryption.
#### Asymmetric vs Symmetric Encryption
Data encryption is employed when you want to protect data from someone you haven't authorized to access or use said data. Data like your health information or your banking details. Encryption works by taking what's known as plaintext (if you can read it, it's in plaintext), running it through some kind of cipher, and outputting it as ciphertext.

In order to not lose the original message, it's useful to use a kind of cipher that is somehow reversible. Ciphers have been used ever since information has been worth stealing. From the simple substitution cipher like the classic Caesar cipher, where letters are shifted by a fixed number, to more complex ones, like the Vigenère cipher, which uses a repeating key to change the shift amount for each letter.

The more complex the cipher used, the more difficult it is to "crack" the ciphertext back into plaintext. In modern times, the ciphers used are mathematical equations that rely on very large prime numbers. The reason for this is outside the scope of this post, and, to be quite honest, are far outside the scope of my current understanding. I'll eventually learn this as well, but feel free to get a head start.

Okay, with the introductions out of the way, let's talk symmetries. Symmetric encryption is basically passwords. You use a password to encrypt something, you use that password to decrypt that something. Just like logging in your favorite social media site. Top-level, what's happening in the background is that you enter your desired password and the encryption algorithm uses that as a kind of key for the thing you are encrypting. Whenever you want to decrypt that thing, you enter the password and the encryption algorithm, like a lock, magically decrypts.

Asymmetric encryption is a bit different in that you generate a key **pair**. Understanding this kind of encryption takes some setting up and mental gymnastics, but is pretty straight forward once you get it. One key "locks" the data and only the other one can "unlock" it. Think of it kind of like a key and padlock, but in this scenario, we're going to keep the key well guarded. If someone wants to send me something secret and wants to make sure only I can see it, they can ask me for a copy of my padlock. I make it for them and send it over. They put the McGuffin in a box and lock it with my padlock, which only I can open (assuming the box can't be broken, yada yada). They now know with certainty that since I'm the only one with the key, they can safely assume only I can see the McGuffin.

Asymmetric encryption has another built-in feature as well. The *private key* that only I have can also act as a kind of "monarch's seal." I can use my key to digitally sign my work. The process is the same as someone putting the McGuffin in the box and sealing it with something that only I can decrypt, but in reverse. My key can also encrypt a McGuffin that anyone can decrypt. Since I'm the only one that can encrypt this McGuffin in a way that my *public key* can decrypt, it must stand to reason that I am the originator of this McGuffin.

The logical next step is discussing how powerful this private key can be. It can sign work digitally and decrypt secret messages intended for a specific person. Sounds like something precious. All things precious make valuable targets for theft. How do we protect the private key? Well, there's no right answer here. Often, the most useful way to protect it is to encrypt it with a password. Yes, you can, and should, combine asymmetric and symmetric encryption. Together, they create a sum that is far greater than the of their parts.
#### Levels of Encryption
What can you do with this? And how does this pertain to tier 3?

There are various levels of encryption that each have their place and use-case. Starting with the highest level, full-disk encryption, or FDE, is used to encrypt your entire hard drive. You may have heard of BitLocker. That's Microsoft's FDE solution. Linux has LUKS and Mac has FileVault. Often, when you lock up the whole disk, you have to enter a password just to boot into it, though not always necessarily. With Windows 11, the hard drive is fully encrypted when the machine is shutdown – always shutdown your device when not in use, especially mobile devices like laptops.

Next step down is partition level encryption. This is pretty self-explanatory for technical people, but for the uninitiated, imagine your hard drive like your house. By default, PC largely ship from the manufacture as what can be related to an open-house design. You have one, big room you can put all your stuff in. You also have the option to partition off different sections of your home for different use-cases; a gaming partition, a work partition, a relaxation partition, and so on. The tools for FDE are the same you use for partition level encryption

You can partition your hard drive in much the same way, though it's easier to do when you first buy the machine than after a bit, especially with Windows, which likes to spread out like a cat on the bed. One partition for your daily driver, one partition for learning and breaking things, a data-only partition. You get the picture. Be aware that partitioning your hard drive is relatively safe, but can still be a risky procedure. ==Always== make sure to ==back up your data== before doing this. Logically, the next step down from FDE is partition-level encryption, which describes encrypting a partition of your hard drive.

Now, let's talk volumes. Instead of a home, imagine a multi-floor office building. Each floor has put up partitions, much like our metaphorical home discussed earlier. The company that owns the more departments than it has space for on a single floor. A few of these departments span multiple floors. The concept of departments and that they can optionally span multiple floors is directly comparable to volumes. You can partition drives, and multiple drives are comparable to multiple floors or buildings. But, with volumes, you can tie drive storage together regardless of which drive it's on.

And finally, we have directories (folders) and files. Zip, 7z, and OS built-in tools are the way to go here. After protecting your drives, it might seem superfluous to then encrypt directories and files. Do not fall into this trap; remember: security is about layers. Encrypting your drives is useful for when that drive is off. This is known in the industry as data-at-rest or encryption-at-rest. Once you turn it on, it is decrypted and becomes data-in-use and anyone that steals it will steal it in its unencrypted state. So, decide what needs to still be protected while the drive is in use and unencrypted and encrypt those things.

There is database and record level encryption, but you will likely never need to know what these are. Briefly, a database is effectively a warehouse for data, a record is an item in that warehouse. A record contains information describing something, an address, a timestamp of when it was added or modified, a status like whether it is active or inactive. Inactive records are ignored by whatever programs – known as jobs – are being run against the database, thereby treating it like it doesn't exist.
#### End-to-End Encryption (E2EE)
Now, we can put this all together to encrypt our data. There are three states of data: data-at-rest, data-in-transit, and data-in-use. To explain these three states, let's imagine a bank transaction at an ATM. Banks have one of the most strict rulesets by which they operate. Everything must be protected, incredibly accurate, and impressively accessible. A breakdown in any one of these has the potential to cost the bank an extraordinary amount. You insert your card and authenticate with your PIN. Then, you can do whatever you need to do with your account; check your account balances, transfer funds between accounts, deposit funds into one or more accounts, or withdraw funds from one or more accounts. Finally, you hit finish, remove your card and any withdrawals, and walk away.

Before and after your transaction, your account information is referred to as data-at-rest, aka inactive data. Your bank is employing various forms of encryption, firewall rules, security software, and data loss prevention methods to keep this safe. It is the least vulnerable state of data, as modifying it in this state requires bypassing all security measures. 

After authenticating yourself, the ATM fetches the requested data. While this data moves to and from the database to the ATM, it is considered data-in-transit, or data-in-motion. Moving data across the internet exposes the data to capture, redirection, and other dangers, making it less secure than data-at-rest. Securing this requires a bit more finesse. Send the data encrypted, then decrypt it at the device using symmetric or asymmetric keys, or a mix of both. Instead of sending the data from the database to the ATM, have the ATM VPN into the bank's network and query the data that way. These are a couple (very simple) examples. Your bank likely uses complex networking and encryption rules to ensure the safety of the data of its members.

Finally, the ATM needs to read the data and after the transaction is finished, the ATM needs to reflect the modifications you wanted to make to the data. Reading, writing, processing, and modifying the data puts this data in the data-in-use state. This is the most vulnerable state for data to be in, as it's directly accessible to an individual. This exposes the data to attack and human error, either of which can have devastating consequences. Encryption is a great start to protecting data-in-use, but this should be reinforce with other methods of protection; authentication with strong passwords, access control lists (ACLs), permissions, up-to-date software, safety awareness trainings, etc.
## Self-Hosting
Okay, I've rambled on and on about how corporations are mining your data. No, they aren't stealing it. No, this isn't a passive process. You are actively giving them legal rights to it. The next  step is to start building out a network under your control with the services that you control. Want a security system? Dump Amazon's cloud-first Blink cameras for [Frigate](https://github.com/blakeblackshear/frigate/). Want a personal media streaming service? Build a [Plex server](https://www.plex.tv/personal-media-server/), then go to your local libraries for CDs, DVDs, and games you can burn and store. This has the added benefit of supporting local libraries instead of megacorporations, like Hulu and Netflix. Be sure to check with the library regarding their rules for this. For legal reasons, I am not a lawyer and I do not condone theft. There is an endless list of self-hosting solutions to replace online services. Search "\[service] self host alternative" and fall down the rabbit hole. Then, use the encryption techniques mentioned in this post to secure it.
